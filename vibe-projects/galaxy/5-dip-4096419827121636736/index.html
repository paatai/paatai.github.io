Candidate 5: an orange dwarf near the Omega Nebula that dimmed and recovered

about 25%

Why this probability. Four instruments see the change, and nearby stars rule out a survey drift. The search lowered this from about 30% because the star may be young, where long dust dimmings are a known behaviour, the fading half rests on Pan-STARRS1 alone, and the star is now brighter than in 2010, so the dip may be a pause in a slow rise. Slow changes of a few tenths of a magnitude are also common among similar dwarfs, often the spotted, fast-spinning ones. This star shows no rotation, which argues against that, but an activity-driven rise and fall cannot be ruled out.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level.

What changed. An orange dwarf in Sagittarius, in the plane of the Milky Way, 1.8 degrees from the Omega Nebula, a region where stars are still forming. Gaia estimates about 5,050 K. It is about 1.5 kiloparsecs (5,000 light-years) away, similar to the distance of that nebula, with about a 10 percent uncertainty, in a crowded field. Pan-STARRS1 saw it fade by about 0.2 magnitudes in green, red and near-infrared light from 2010 to 2014, or by 0.4 magnitudes in green if a single 2009 measurement is included. SkyMapper from 2014 to 2021, Gaia from late 2014 to mid-2017, and ZTF from 2018 to 2025 then followed a rise of about 0.5 magnitudes, which ended around 2022. It has been flat since. Where the surveys overlap they agree. The star is now about 0.1 magnitudes brighter than the 2009 measurement and 0.2 to 0.35 magnitudes brighter than in 2010 to 2012.

Why it might matter. Ordinary quiet dwarf stars do not dim and recover by half a magnitude over a decade. The star is quiet from night to night: the ZTF scatter equals the measurement errors, and there is no rotation period. Its Gaia spectrum shows no hydrogen emission. The dimming was deeper in blue light than in red, as dust would cause. A dusty cloud passing in front of the star is one explanation. The pattern can also be read as a slow brightening with a dip around 2014.

SIMBAD shows nothing known. The variable-star index lists the star only through an automatic import of Gaia’s machine classification. Gaia alerts, published variable-star lists and arXiv show nothing. Gaia’s images show a single star, despite 15 fainter stars within 10 arcseconds. The three nearest are circled on the finder. About 30 nearby stars of similar brightness did not fade like this in Pan-STARRS1, and 22 did not change like this in ZTF.

Caveat
It may be a young star. Long dust dimmings are a known behaviour of young stars with dusty discs. The star lies 0.7 magnitudes above the main sequence for its colour, which fits a young star or an unresolved binary, and it is at about the distance of the Omega Nebula’s star-forming region. It shows no hydrogen emission. The infrared images are too blended to test for warm dust. The fading half rests on Pan-STARRS1 alone. The three 2014 measurements come from one poor night, each uncertain by 0.2 magnitudes, although SkyMapper confirms that level a month later. The level before 2009 is unknown. No spectrum exists in the public archives the search checked. Gaia’s automatic classifier calls it a spotted active star, with a low score.
