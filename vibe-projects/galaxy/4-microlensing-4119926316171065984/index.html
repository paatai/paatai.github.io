Candidate 4: a bulge star that brightened at least two-fold

Promising

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Orange shading marks the change. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level.

Dates such as 2016.22 are a year plus a fraction of a year. 2016.00 is the start of 2016, and 2016.50 is about halfway through the year.

What changed. A star in Sagittarius, 4.8 degrees above the Milky Way’s mid-plane and about 10 degrees from the Galactic centre. It is far away, its parallax too small to measure, and it is reddened by dust. The field is crowded: five other Gaia stars lie within 6 arcseconds. Gaia’s measurements begin in September 2014, and the star was already 0.44 magnitudes brighter than normal. At 2015.15 it was 0.83 magnitudes brighter, 2.1 times the light. Then it faded: 0.55 magnitudes brighter at 2015.22, 0.34 at 2015.29, and back to normal by mid-2015. It stayed steady until Gaia’s published data end, at 2017.3. ZTF saw nothing from 2018 to 2025. Gaia’s blue, green and red bands brightened by the same amount. The peak itself fell in a gap between Gaia visits around the turn of 2014 and 2015, so the maximum brightness is unknown.

WISE cannot separate this star from a brighter neighbour 5.5 arcseconds away (Gaia G = 16.9, circled on the finder), so it measures the pair as one blurred source. This star gives about 40 percent of that source’s infrared light, judged from where WISE places the blend: normally about 3.4 arcseconds from this star, toward the neighbour. WISE visited twice during the event, in September 2014 and March 2015. Both times the blended source was 0.18 to 0.26 magnitudes brighter than on every comparable visit from 2010 to 2020, which agree to within 0.04 to 0.08 magnitudes, and the blend sat closer to this star, at 2.2 arcseconds. That is what should happen if this star brightened by the factor Gaia measured. The lens model predicts 0.19 to 0.24 magnitudes for the blend. Position alone would not be decisive: one ordinary visit in 2019 also placed the source close to this star. Only the two event visits are both brighter and shifted.

Why it might matter. A foreground star passing almost exactly in front of a distant star focuses its light, so the distant star brightens and fades once, smoothly, and by the same factor in every colour. Here the standard lens model fits all 53 Gaia measurements more closely than a plain bump. The Gaia colours and the infrared agree, and nothing similar shows in WISE, Gaia or ZTF from 2010 to 2025. Gaia’s automatic classifier labelled it microlensing.

It may be new. It is not in Gaia’s published list of 363 microlensing events. It has no Gaia alert, and no entry in SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index) or the Transient Name Server. The search found nothing on arXiv. The star lies outside the fields of the OGLE early-warning system. KMTNet, which began full operations in 2016, did not list it. It is not in OGLE’s list of Galactic-disk events (Mroz et al. 2020), and it sits just outside OGLE’s nearest disk fields. A microlensing event is an expected kind of event, not an exotic one.

The search first rated this one weak, on 31 October 2026, and raised it to promising on 1 November 2026 after the WISE check. The field is crowded, and Gaia’s brightness measurements rise and fall with one of Gaia’s image-quality indicators, which could have meant a measurement artefact from the neighbours. The WISE result shows the light change was real and came from this star’s position. A real brightening among close neighbours can also produce that correlation.

Caveat
The peak was not observed, so the maximum and the lens parameters are uncertain. The WISE amplitude depends on how WISE splits the blend, and on one visit, the 3.4-micron band in March 2015, it split the blend differently. No public optical survey imaged this field during the event: none of the NOIRLab Dark Energy Camera archive, SkyMapper or ZTF has data from those months. WISE’s images are too blurry, about 6 arcseconds, to show the star separately.
