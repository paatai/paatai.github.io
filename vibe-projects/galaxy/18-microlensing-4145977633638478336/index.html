Candidate 18: a star that brightened by 60% for about two months

Promising (as a new microlensing event)

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Orange shading marks the change. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level.

Dates such as 2016.22 are a year plus a fraction of a year. 2016.00 is the start of 2016, and 2016.50 is about halfway through the year.

What changed. A distant star in Serpens, 2.2 degrees above the Milky Way’s mid-plane toward the inner Galaxy, 14 degrees from the Galactic centre. The parallax is 0.11 ± 0.05 milliarcseconds, so the star is several thousand parsecs away, in a crowded field. Gaia measured it 28 times between 2014 and 2017. It was steady except in early 2016: 0.46 magnitudes brighter at 2016.18, 0.36 at 2016.26, 0.15 at 2016.32, and normal again by 2016.7. In each of those visits the blue, green and red bands agree to 0.05 magnitudes.

Two other telescopes caught it. NEOWISE, at 2016.22, close to the peak, found it 0.52 magnitudes brighter at 3.4 microns and 0.56 magnitudes brighter at 4.6 microns. SkyMapper, at 2016.30, found it 0.19 to 0.23 magnitudes brighter in r, i and z, when Gaia had it about 0.20 magnitudes brighter. NEOWISE watched twice a year from 2014 to 2024, and ZTF from 2018 to 2025. Neither saw anything else.

Why it might matter. Gravitational lensing brightens a star once, smoothly, and by the same factor at every wavelength. This star brightened by the same amount, between 0.2 and 0.5 magnitudes depending on the date, in eight bands from blue light to 4.6 microns, and only once in ten years. Outbursts and flares change colour strongly. The search knows of no ordinary variable star that brightens like this. Gaia’s automatic classifier labelled it microlensing.

It may be new. It is not in Gaia’s published list of 363 microlensing events. It has no Gaia alert, and no entry in SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index) or the Transient Name Server. The search found nothing on arXiv. It is not in the OGLE or KMTNet microlensing lists for 2014 to 2018, nor in OGLE’s published list of Galactic-disk events (Mroz et al. 2020). A microlensing event is an expected kind of event, not an exotic one.

Caveat
Gaia caught the event in only three visits, five measurements, so the shape alone cannot prove lensing. A plain symmetric bump fits as well, and the lens timescale, about 30 days, is only a rough guess. The case rests on the colours agreeing and on three instruments seeing the same thing. The star sits 0.4 degrees from the centre of an OGLE field, BLG767, imaged 80 times over about three years, which may or may not have covered early 2016. Four faint stars, G from 19.7 to 20.6, lie within 5 arcseconds, and a star of G = 17.4 lies 6.9 arcseconds away. WISE cannot separate them, but the infrared brightening matches Gaia’s, so the light change belongs to this star.
