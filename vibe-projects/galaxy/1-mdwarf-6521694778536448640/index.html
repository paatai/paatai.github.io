Candidate 1: a red dwarf that faded for about a year

about 60%

Why this probability. The fading is certainly real: Gaia, the Dark Energy Camera and SkyMapper all saw it. Without a spectrum the cause is still open. The grey colour argues against starspots only weakly, and only the star’s motion argues that it is not a young star with a dusty disc.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Orange shading marks the change. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level. The last two figures include difference images, one picture of the sky subtracted from another. A blue spot means the star was fainter than in the comparison picture.

What changed. This is a cool red dwarf in the southern constellation Phoenix, smaller and cooler than the Sun, and far too faint to see by eye. Gaia measured it 99 times between 2014 and May 2017. It was steady until late 2016, apart from one slightly faint visit in February 2016. From November 2016 until Gaia’s published measurements end, in May 2017, it was 0.1 to 0.5 magnitudes fainter. That is 7 to 38 percent less light, and the depth could change by up to 0.2 magnitudes within two or three days.

Two other telescopes saw the same fading. On 4 November 2016 the Dark Energy Camera in Chile found the star 0.105 ± 0.007 magnitudes fainter in its z filter, and 0.083 ± 0.015 magnitudes fainter in Y, than in earlier images. The number after ± is the uncertainty. An image from 3 October 2016 was normal, so the fading began during those four weeks. On 13 November 2016 SkyMapper, in Australia, found it about 0.2 magnitudes fainter than its own later measurements.

The return to normal is uneven. On 12 August 2017 SkyMapper had it only 0.03 to 0.06 magnitudes faint, but on 7 October 2017 it was 0.1 to 0.15 magnitudes faint again, in two filters. A Dark Energy Camera z image on 28 September 2017 also shows it about 0.11 magnitudes faint, though the star sits at the edge of the detector there, so that value is less reliable. Each of these 2017 points is only a weak measurement on its own. The star was back to normal by May 2018 in SkyMapper and by September 2018 in the Dark Energy Camera. A SkyMapper image from June 2021 is normal too.

The search re-measured the star on 15 usable deep SkyMapper images, including six r-filter images from August and September 2017 and May 2018 that the SkyMapper catalogue had mostly not measured. The bright companion’s light spills into the star in SkyMapper’s blurrier images, so the search subtracts it, using the same distance from the companion in other directions. Where the catalogue also measured the star, the two agree to within 0.07 magnitudes. On average the new r points sit at the normal level, 0.04 ± 0.04 magnitudes fainter than expected from the Dark Energy Camera after allowing for the different filters. They scatter more than their errors, by about 0.4 magnitudes between nights 11 days apart. That could be flickering, but noise would do it about once in 50 tries, so the search does not call it significant.

There is a second, shorter dip. A Dark Energy Camera image on 15 November 2018 shows the star 0.095 ± 0.007 magnitudes faint in the i filter. The archive catalogue gives 0.093 for that same image. The value does not change with the size of the measuring window (0.089 to 0.099 across six sizes), and there are no bad pixels near the star. Twelve days later it was nearly normal: 0.02 ± 0.01 magnitudes faint in r and 0.04 ± 0.03 in g. All 60 i-filter images from July and from October and November 2019 are within 0.03 magnitudes of normal. This second dip rests on a single image, so it is less certain than the main fading.

The colour of the fade is a clue. In Gaia the fading was about as deep in the red band as in the broad band (a ratio of 1.09 ± 0.11; fine interstellar dust would give about 0.83). SkyMapper’s near-infrared z filter, at 0.9 microns, showed about the same 0.2 magnitudes as Gaia and SkyMapper’s i filter a few days apart. From blue light to the near infrared the fading was roughly grey. The preference for that over fine dust is only a hint, about 2.4 times the uncertainty, not a proof.

In the infrared the fading was much weaker. WISE measured the star at 3.4 microns twice during the fading, on 3 to 10 November 2016 and on 20 to 27 May 2017, using the combined image of each visit. Both times it was only 0.05 ± 0.05 magnitudes fainter than in 2010 to 2016, while visible and near-infrared light were about 0.2 magnitudes fainter. A grey fade would have shown about 0.2 magnitudes in WISE too. That is ruled out, at about three times the uncertainty. An opaque body covering part of the star would dim every colour equally, so this points to a thin veil of dust. A standard light-scattering calculation puts the grains at a few tenths of a micron across, about 0.2 to 0.5 microns: bigger than most interstellar grains, which would redden the light, but small enough to let 3.4-micron light through. Grains of a micron or more would dim the infrared at least as much as the visible light. A similar pattern, nearly the same in visible colours and weaker at 3.6 to 4.5 microns, was reported for the slow fading of Boyajian’s star and put down to dust (Meng et al. 2017). That is a comparison of colours only. Boyajian’s star is hotter, and its slow fading was a few percent, not 20 percent.

Why it might matter. Red dwarfs do flare, in brief brightenings, and they do have starspots, which make small regular wiggles. They do not normally fade by up to a third for a year. A long fading is usually dust passing in front of the star, for example a dusty disc around an unseen companion. Young stars often do this. This one is probably old. The system lies about 580 parsecs below the plane of the Milky Way, where young stars are rare, and it moves like an ordinary older disc star. Old red dwarfs are not expected to have dusty discs, which is what makes the event unusual.

A Sun-like star 14 arcseconds to the south, the bright star below the target on the finder, is at the same distance (parallaxes 1.53 and 1.60 milliarcseconds) and moves the same way on the sky (the motions differ by 0.5 ± 0.2 milliarcseconds per year). It is about 175 times brighter, at Gaia G = 13.7. An unrelated star this close and this similar would turn up by chance only about once in a thousand fields like this. Gaia’s automatic analysis gives it a temperature of about 5,800 K, like the Sun, perhaps somewhat fewer heavy elements than the Sun (uncertain), and a speed along the line of sight of 13.4 ± 2.5 km/s. The two stars are at least 9,200 AU apart, about 300 times the Sun–Neptune distance. The companionship is statistical. The red dwarf does not yet have a matching line-of-sight speed.

SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index) and the Gaia alerts list show nothing known apart from an automatic Gaia entry. Gaia’s indicators of instrumental artefacts are normal. Other stars nearby did not fade on the same dates. Infrared colours from 2MASS, VHS and WISE are normal for a red dwarf: no sign of warm dust, although cold or faint dust would not show. The search re-measured about 110 archived Dark Energy Camera images in five filters and 15 SkyMapper images, and checked 16 WISE visits from 2010 to 2020 against hundreds of field stars of the same brightness. GALEX, in the ultraviolet, and eROSITA, in X-rays, do not detect the star. That is expected at this distance and tells nothing.

Caveat
The main event is sparsely sampled, about 30 measurements from three telescopes, and the depth varies between dates. There is no spectrum. The 2018 dip rests on one image, and the autumn 2017 dip on three marginal measurements. The companion is not confirmed by a matching line-of-sight speed for the red dwarf. The infrared test assumes the visible-light depth during each WISE visit matched the measurements just before and after it; the depth could change by 0.2 magnitudes within days. WISE also reads the star about 0.1 magnitudes faint in 2019 and 2020, when the Dark Energy Camera saw it normal. Each of those points is under twice its uncertainty, so the search does not count them.
