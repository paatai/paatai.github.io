Candidate 53: a red giant that dimmed for a year

about 25%

Why this estimate. The dimming is real: ZTF flagged it 13 times, and ASAS-SN, a separate network of small telescopes, saw the same dip in two of its cameras. Green light dimmed more than red and the infrared did not change, which argues against starspots. But the star is a giant and probably has a companion, and a machine classification calls it a spotted binary.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart shows a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds long. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude, a brightness scale on which larger numbers mean fainter stars, and it is drawn so that down means fainter. Purple shading marks the dimming. Each survey uses slightly different filters, so each is compared with its own normal level. The ZTF pictures show ZTF’s reference image of the star, an image taken during the dimming, and the difference between the two, in which only what changed remains: blue means fainter and red brighter.

What changed. The star is a red giant of about 4,800 K and about five times the Sun’s radius, 1,400 to 1,800 parsecs away in Andromeda. ZTF saw it steady from 2018 to 2021, about 0.01 magnitudes faint in green light during 2022, and then 0.06 to 0.08 magnitudes fainter in green and 0.03 to 0.05 in red from spring 2023 to early 2024, about 7 per cent less light. It was back to normal by mid-2024. ASAS-SN saw the same dip from June to December 2023.

Why it might matter. Blue light dimmed about 1.7 times as much as red light, more than ordinary interstellar dust (1.4) or starspots (about 1.1 for a star this cool). WISE observed the star in July and December 2023, deep in the dip, and saw no change at 3.4 microns, which excludes a solid body and is less than starspots would give. Gaia’s measurements of the star scatter more than those of a single star (its RUWE is 1.6), which suggests a companion, and dust in such a pair could pass in front of the giant.

No record of the dimming could be found. The star is not in SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index), Gaia’s variable-star lists or the other catalogues consulted, nor among the dimming stars of Tzanidakis et al. (2025), and there is no Transient Name Server entry, Gaia alert or paper giving its Gaia number. ZTF’s alert system holds only machine-generated classifications for it. One of them calls it a spotted binary of the RS Canum Venaticorum kind, with a probability of about one half.
