Candidate 10: a star that brightened four-fold for a few weeks

about 45%

Why this probability. Five Gaia visits trace a clean lensing curve, but the only independent detection is a single WISE visit, and two close neighbours contaminate the colour and the infrared measurements.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Orange shading marks the change. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level.

Dates such as 2016.22 are a year plus a fraction of a year. 2016.00 is the start of 2016, and 2016.50 is about halfway through the year.

What changed. A faint star in Cygnus, 3.6 degrees from the Milky Way’s mid-plane. Its parallax, Gaia’s distance measure, is 0.09 ± 0.23 milliarcseconds, too small to measure, so the star is probably more than 2,000 parsecs away. Gaia measured it 59 times between 2014 and 2017. It was steady except in early 2016, when five visits caught it rising and falling: 0.5 magnitudes brighter at 2016.16, 1.5 magnitudes brighter (four times the light) at 2016.22, 0.5 magnitudes at 2016.32, almost normal at 2016.42, and normal by 2016.47. ZTF watched from 2018 to 2025 and the star never did this again.

WISE happened to visit at 2016.35. Its single exposures showed the star 0.21 ± 0.11 magnitudes bright, only a weak detection. The deeper combined image of that visit gives 0.19 ± 0.03 magnitudes bright, which is what the lens model predicts, and it is the brightest of 16 visits from 2010 to 2020. Stars of the same brightness in this field vary by about 0.05 magnitudes from visit to visit, and only about 1 in 200 of their measurements is that bright. One infrared point cannot show the shape of the event.

Why it might matter. When a foreground star, or a dark object, passes almost exactly in front of a distant star, its gravity focuses the distant star’s light. The distant star then brightens and fades once, symmetrically, and by the same factor in every colour. That is what this light curve looks like. The standard lens model fits all 59 points, and a plain bump fits worse. The red and blue Gaia bands rise and fall together, and there is no repeat. Gaia’s automatic classifier also labelled it microlensing.

It may be a new one. It is not in Gaia’s published list of 363 microlensing events. It has no Gaia alert, and no entry in SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index) or the Transient Name Server. It lies far outside the fields of the OGLE and KMTNet microlensing surveys. A microlensing event is an expected kind of event, not an exotic one. Its value would be as one more lens toward the disc of the Milky Way.

Caveat
Gaia is the only telescope that sampled the shape of the event. One other WISE visit, at 2020.35, reads 0.16 magnitudes faint, so the infrared measurements have occasional outliers. Two fainter stars 2.7 and 2.8 arcseconds away add light in Gaia’s red and blue bands and in WISE, though they are outside Gaia’s measuring window for G. At 2016.16, two measurements 106 minutes apart disagree by 0.4 magnitudes in the red band but agree in G. No lensed star changes that fast, so that red point is a glitch, and the colour fits are less tidy than the G fit. The peak at 2016.22 is consistent in all three bands, and Gaia flagged none of the brightening measurements as bad or noisy. Gaia’s earlier data release does not show the brightening in the average brightness, but the same is true of two events OGLE confirmed, so that test does not work for an event this short and this bright.
