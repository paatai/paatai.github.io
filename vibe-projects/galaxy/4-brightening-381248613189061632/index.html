Candidate 4: an orange dwarf that brightened for a decade

about 30%

Why this probability. The decade-long rise is certainly real. The search lowered this from about 70% to about 30% after ZTF showed a small repeating wobble, most likely every 1.19 days, marking a fast-spinning, magnetically active star, probably a close binary. Other spotted active stars changed by comparable amounts, so a giant starspot cycle is now the likelier cause. Dust is still favoured by the one-way rise after decades at one level and by a normal ultraviolet brightness while the star was faint.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart is a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude. A larger magnitude means a fainter star, so the axis runs downwards: down means fainter. Orange shading marks the change. Surveys use slightly different filters, so each one is compared with its own normal level.

What changed. An orange dwarf, a K star, cooler and smaller than the Sun, in the constellation Andromeda. It lies 0.8 degrees northwest of the centre of the Andromeda Galaxy, but it is a Milky Way star in front of that galaxy, not part of it. Gaia estimates a temperature of about 4,900 K and a low content of heavy elements, about a tenth of the Sun’s. It is about 1.3 to 1.9 kiloparsecs away (4,000 to 6,000 light-years) and 500 to 700 parsecs below the plane of the Milky Way, so it is probably old.

For at least 20 years the star stayed at one faint level. Photographic plates from 1983, 1989 and 1993, the 2MASS survey in 2000, and the Sloan survey in 2002 all agree. From about 2010 it brightened steadily. Four surveys followed the rise: Pan-STARRS1 from 2009 to 2014, the Palomar Transient Factory from 2010 to 2014, Gaia from 2014 to 2017, and ZTF from 2018 to 2025. By 2020 it was 0.6 to 0.7 magnitudes brighter in green light and 0.5 to 0.7 magnitudes brighter in red light, which is 60 to 90 percent more light. Since 2020 the rise has almost stopped, about 0.01 magnitudes per year. In the infrared, at 3.4 microns, it brightened by only about 0.2 magnitudes. The 1953 plates are less precise, and they disagree with each other: the blue plate shows the faint level, and the red plate a level between faint and bright.

Why it might matter. Ordinary, slowly spinning dwarf stars do not brighten by more than half a magnitude over a decade. Magnetic activity cycles change such stars by a few hundredths of a magnitude up to about 0.1, and the change repeats. This change is several times larger and has gone one way only. Within each survey the star became bluer as it brightened: blue light rose faster than red. From night to night it varies only slightly, 0.001 to 0.006 magnitudes within a month in Gaia and about 0.02 in ZTF. Searched one observing season at a time, though, ZTF from 2018 to 2025 shows a small repeating wobble of about 2 percent, larger in green than in red, as starspots produce. The same period comes back in 9 of 16 season-and-colour sets. None of 30 nearby stars of the same brightness, searched the same way, repeats a period in more than five. The period is most likely 1.19 days. Because ZTF observes once a night, 0.86 days and 6.1 days cannot be excluded. Any of these means the star rotates fast, so it is magnetically active and spotted. An old K dwarf spins this fast only if a close companion keeps it spinning, so it is probably a close binary.

Two explanations remain. Dust clearing: a dusty cloud or disc that had dimmed the star for decades moved out of the line of sight. Ordinary interstellar dust would have changed the colours more than observed; dust with a mixture of small and large grains fits. A change in the star itself: the colours also fit a hotter surface or fewer cool dark spots, and so does the small infrared change. The star would have to be about 550 K hotter, 11 percent, which means about 50 percent more light, or to have lost dark spots covering half to nine tenths of its surface. Ordinary dwarf stars do neither in a decade. Fast-spinning, magnetically active dwarfs, often close binaries, are the exception: their average brightness drifts over decades as the spot cover changes. Against that reading, the star had normal ultraviolet brightness in the 2000s, while it was still faint, when spots should have been strongest, and its Gaia spectrum shows no hydrogen-alpha emission, a weak test at this brightness. It also rose one way for a decade after at least 20 years at one level, rather than going up and down.

Compared with 425 similar dwarf stars, a starspot cycle is now the likelier explanation. Ten changed by 0.45 magnitudes or more from Pan-STARRS1 to ZTF, and seven of those ten spin fast, this star among them. Against field stars of the same colour, two fast-spinning dwarfs brightened by 0.3 to 0.4 magnitudes over those years, and two spotted active binaries by 0.6 to 0.8. On the same measure this star brightened by about 0.5 magnitudes. Dust is still favoured by the one-way rise after decades at one level and by the normal ultraviolet brightness while the star was faint. Nothing shows the dust directly.

SIMBAD and VSX (the variable-star index) show nothing known. Gaia’s variability summary assigns no class. Gaia’s fit to the motion on the sky is that of a normal single star (its single-star statistic, RUWE, is 1.02). There is no other Gaia source within 15 arcseconds, and no star moving with it within 2 arcminutes. Catalogues from ROSAT, XMM-Newton and Chandra for this part of the sky, and Swift ultraviolet catalogues, show nothing. An XMM-Newton image from 2017 does not detect the star, so it is not a strong X-ray source: the limit is below about 1030 erg/s. Its GALEX ultraviolet brightness is normal for an orange dwarf of this colour, compared with 238 similar stars, so there is no sign of strong activity or of a hot companion. Forty-one stars of similar brightness within 6 arcminutes do not show the trend in the Palomar Transient Factory, so it is not a calibration drift. Measured directly against two steady neighbours in the same images, the star brightens by the same amount as in the survey catalogues.

Caveat
No spectrum exists in the public archives the search checked, LAMOST and the Sloan survey, so the type of the star and the cause of the change rest on Gaia’s colours. The faint level before 2010 comes from photographic plates, precise to 0.05 to 0.12 magnitudes, and one Sloan visit. An independent scan of the same plates agrees within the errors. The rotation period is uncertain (1.19, 0.86 or 6.1 days). A rare kind of variable star that behaves like this may have been missed.
