Candidate 48: a Sun-like star that dimmed for a year

about 35%

Why this estimate. The dimming is real: ZTF recorded it on two of its detectors and ASAS-SN, a separate network of small telescopes, saw it in each of its five cameras that observed the star. Green light dimmed about twice as much as red, as behind fine dust, and NASA’s TESS space telescope found no sign that the star is spotted. The dip is shallow, the infrared cannot decide between spots and dust, and there is no spectrum.

How to read the pictures. The finder chart shows a patch of sky two arcminutes across, about a fifteenth of the Moon’s width, with north up and east to the left. The star sits between the two yellow ticks, and the white bar is 30 arcseconds long. On a light curve the vertical axis is magnitude, a brightness scale on which larger numbers mean fainter stars, and it is drawn so that down means fainter. Purple shading marks the dimming. Each survey uses slightly different filters, so each is compared with its own normal level. The ZTF pictures show ZTF’s reference image of the star, an image taken during the dimming, and the difference between the two, in which only what changed remains: blue means fainter and red brighter. On the TESS light curves the star is black and the comparison stars are coloured.

What changed. The star is a Sun-like dwarf, about 5,800 K and 6.7 billion years old, some 810 parsecs away in Cepheus. ZTF saw it steady from 2018 to early 2023. From May 2023 it dimmed by 0.04 to 0.06 magnitudes in green light, reached 0.063 in green and 0.033 in red in February and March 2024, about 6 per cent less light, and was back to normal by June 2024. ZTF’s alert system flagged it 7 times, and ASAS-SN saw the star 0.03 to 0.07 magnitudes faint in green light in every month from June to December 2023.

Why it might matter. Blue light dimmed about twice as much as red light, more than ordinary interstellar dust (1.4) or starspots (1.1 to 1.3) would cause and as fine dust would. TESS watched the star in many sectors outside the dip and never saw the regular wave of a spotted star. WISE observed twice during the dip and saw the star at most a few thousandths of a magnitude fainter at 3.4 microns, too little to tell dust from spots at this depth.

No record of the dimming could be found. The star is not in SIMBAD, VSX (the variable-star index), Gaia’s variable-star lists or the other catalogues consulted, nor among the dimming stars of Tzanidakis et al. (2025), and there is no Transient Name Server entry, Gaia alert or paper giving its Gaia number. ZTF’s alert system holds only machine-generated classifications for it.
