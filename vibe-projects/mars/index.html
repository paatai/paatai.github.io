Possible new impact sites on Mars

NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed much of Mars with its Context Camera (CTX) several times since 2006, and when a small meteorite hits dusty ground it blows the bright dust away and leaves a dark spot that CTX can see, even when the crater itself is only a few metres wide. This search compared old and new CTX images of the same ground automatically, pixel by pixel, and kept only dark spots that appeared between two dates and are missing from the published catalogue of 1,203 dated new craters (Daubar et al. 2022) and from the camera teams’ own target lists. All but one also lie outside every footprint of the high-resolution HiRISE camera, which photographed candidate 4 by chance. Thousands of image pairs were screened and almost everything flagged was a look-alike (shadows of old craters under a different sun, dark streaks on slopes and dunes, haze, or alignment errors); the fourteen candidates below survived every check. After the first three come seven strong candidates, then four promising ones.

1. Arsia Sulci, west of Arsia Mons

Location: 5.772° S, 230.935° E (129.065° W). Not there: 5 March 2007, 5 July 2016, 18 August 2017 and 2 October 2022. There: 27 April 2026 (image W09_092578_1739).

A round dark spot about 70 m across, inside a fainter halo about 150 m across, appears in the April 2026 image. The same ground shows nothing in four earlier images, taken with the Sun both higher and lower in the sky, so it is not a shadow. Its compact shape and soft halo match the dark blast zones that fresh impacts leave on dusty ground. As a check, the same pair of images also re-found a known impact site 16 km to the south that the HiRISE team photographed in 2026.

Confidence: one of the strongest candidates. That the ground changed is very likely; that it is a new impact is likely, roughly 3 in 4. The weak point is that only one image shows it so far, because no newer CTX image has been released.

2. Plains near Phlegra Dorsa, eastern Elysium

Location: 24.886° N, 161.419° E. Not there: 24 November 2016, 5 April 2018, 13 August 2020 and 22 June 2022. There: 11 May 2026 (image W10_092765_2031).

A compact dark spot about 70 m across appears in the May 2026 image. It is absent in the earlier images, three of which have nearly the same sun angle as the 2026 image, so it is not a shadow. A known cluster of new craters lies about 10 km away, but that cluster formed in 2016–2018, and this spot is still absent in the 2018–2022 images, so it is a separate, later event. No HiRISE footprint covers it; the nearest is 6 km away.

Confidence: promising, roughly 60% that it is a new impact. The spot is fainter than the first candidate, the earlier images are noisy, and only one image shows it.

3. South-east of Gordii Dorsum, south of Olympus Mons

Location: 0.906° N, 227.461° E. Not there: 20 March 2011. There: 21 June 2020, then fainter in 2024 and in March 2026.

A dark spot about 40 m across appears some time between 2011 and 2020. It then fades steadily, which is how impact blast zones behave as dust settles back over them. It is seen at the same place in four separate images (2020, twice in 2024, 2026), so it is not a camera glitch, and it is seen under a higher sun than the 2011 image where it is absent, so it is not a shadow. The nearest HiRISE footprint is 38 km away and the nearest catalogued new crater is 54 km away.

Confidence: promising, roughly 55% that it is an impact. The main ordinary alternative is wind stripping dust from a small patch of this wind-carved terrain.

Seven strong candidates

4. Arabia Terra, north-west of Pasteur crater

Location: 25.452° N, 20.908° E. Not there: 28 November 2010. There: 10 February 2025, 12 December 2025 (CTX and HiRISE) and 24 April 2026.

A dark splash about 30 m across appeared on the dusty plains of Arabia Terra between 2010 and 2025. By chance, NASA’s HiRISE camera photographed it in December 2025. HiRISE shows a sharp-edged dark patch with finger-like margins on flat ground, with a tiny dark point at its edge: just what the blast zone of a fresh, very small crater looks like. Under the same sun angle in 2010, there was nothing there.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 80% that it is a new impact.

5. Cerberus plains, Elysium Planitia

Location: 3.723° N, 166.755° E. Not there: 10 October 2010. There: four images from 8 October 2023 to 14 November 2025.

A dark spot 100 to 150 m across, with dark rays fanning out from a cluster of dark blobs, sits on the flat lava plains of Cerberus. It is identical in four images taken over two years, and absent in 2010 under the same lighting. A rayed pattern around several dark centres is how a small meteoroid that broke apart just before impact typically looks from orbit.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 70% that it is a new impact.

6. Twin dark spot on the plains of Elysium Planitia

Location: 6.089° N, 157.220° E. Not there: 3 October 2006, 4 December 2006 and 27 January 2008. There: 11 August 2024, 22 December 2024 and 16 December 2025.

Two small dark spots, each about 20 m across and 35 m apart, appeared together on smooth plains between 2008 and 2024. An older dark spot nearby shows clearly in the earliest images, so those images would have caught the pair. Two spots appearing at once is what happens when a meteoroid breaks in two in the thin Martian air.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 75% that it is a new impact.

7. West of the volcano Albor Tholus, Elysium Planitia

Location: 16.884° N, 147.869° E. Not there: 22 May 2014 and 28 July 2022. There: four images from 10 April 2025 to 28 November 2025.

A compact, very dark spot 25 to 30 m across appeared on these lava plains between mid-2022 and April 2025. A fainter new companion lies 195 m away. The spot is absent in 2014 under exactly the same sun angle, and unchanged in four images over seven months. Two new spots this close together are the usual sign of an impactor that fragmented on the way down.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 80% that it is a new impact.

8. Daedalia Planum, south-west of Arsia Mons

Location: 6.868° S, 229.425° E (130.575° W). Not there: 22 February 2008 and 30 November 2020. There: 8 December 2024 and 10 November 2025.

A very dark, lobed spot 50 to 60 m across appeared on the lava plains of Daedalia Planum, west of Aganippe Fossa, between 2020 and 2024. It is absent in 2020 under nearly the same lighting, and it keeps the same shape in images 11 months apart. Its size and darkness match the blast zones of new impacts found elsewhere on Mars.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 80% that it is a new impact.

9. Plains south of Acheron Fossae

Location: 35.513° N, 230.446° E (129.554° W). Not there: 23 March 2007, 25 August 2009 and 11 May 2016. There: 1 September 2025 and 26 October 2025.

A dark patch 100 to 120 m across with a darker centre appeared on these northern plains between 2016 and 2025. It is absent in three earlier images, one of them under the same sun angle, and unchanged in two 2025 images taken from different viewing angles. Both sightings were in northern summer, so seasonal frost cannot explain it.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 70% that it is a new impact.

10. West of Albor Tholus, 13 km from candidate 7

Location: 16.949° N, 147.642° E. Not there: 22 May 2014. There: seven images from 28 July 2022 to 28 September 2025.

A round dark spot 35 to 40 m across has sat unchanged on these lava plains in seven images since July 2022. An image from 2014, taken under exactly the same sun angle, shows nothing there. It lies just 1 km outside a 2022 HiRISE image, so it has never been photographed at high resolution.

Confidence: strong candidate; roughly 80% that it is a new impact.

Four promising candidates

11. Lava plains of Ulysses Fossae, between Olympus Mons and Ascraeus Mons

Location: 14.287° N, 235.889° E (124.111° W). Not there: 26 November 2010 and 4 January 2011. There: 29 November 2025.

A diffuse dark patch 100 to 150 m across, with two or three darker blobs inside, appeared on these heavily dust-covered lava plains some time in the last 15 years. Two earlier images taken under the same sun show nothing at the spot. Only one image shows it so far, so it is rated promising rather than strong. Confidence: promising; roughly 55% that it is a new impact.

12. Arabia Terra, 4.6 km from candidate 4

Location: 25.388° N, 20.958° E. Not there: 28 November 2010. There: 10 February 2025 and 12 December 2025 (faint on 24 April 2026).

A small dark spot, about 20 to 25 m across, appeared in the same time window as candidate 4, only 4.6 km away. It may be a second fragment of the same impactor or a separate impact. But it is faint, and it sits at the end of an older dark streak, so it could also be that streak growing. Confidence: promising; roughly 45% that it is a new impact.

13. Arabia Terra, north-west of Schroeter crater

Location: 7.364° N, 51.099° E. Not there: 2 April 2012 and 20 March 2014. There: 15 July 2025.

A sharp, very dark dot 30 to 35 m across appeared on these plains some time between 2014 and 2025. The two earlier images were taken under similar light and show a fainter, older dark feature nearby, so they would have shown the dot. Only one image shows it so far. Confidence: promising; roughly 60% that it is a new impact.

14. Arabia Terra, north-west of Dawes crater

Location: 3.306° S, 33.715° E. Not there: ten images from 26 February 2010 to 20 August 2024. There: 6 July 2025.

A large, diffuse dark oval about 250 × 150 m appeared on these dusty plains within a window of about ten months, between August 2024 and July 2025. It may be the blast zone of a new impact, and three recorded new impacts lie within 31 km. But dust devils are active here and could also scour such a patch, so it is rated promising. Confidence: promising; roughly 45% that it is a new impact.

Caveat

These are unconfirmed candidates, not discoveries. Each one passed every test that could be applied to the existing images, but only a new, sharper image (ideally from HiRISE) can show whether a fresh crater sits at the centre of the spot. The orbiter’s camera teams screen new images routinely and may already know about some of these. “Not in the record” means not in the public archives as of their latest releases: CTX images through May 2026 and HiRISE images through August 2026. One further catalogue, of new impacts near the InSight lander (Bickel et al. 2025), could not be downloaded; of candidates 4 to 14, numbers 5, 6, 7 and 10 lie in its area, and for them it can only be inferred that it does not list them.
