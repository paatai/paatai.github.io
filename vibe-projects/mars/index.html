Possible new impact sites on Mars

NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed much of Mars with its Context Camera (CTX) several times since 2006, and when a small meteorite hits dusty ground it blows the bright dust away and leaves a dark spot that CTX can see, even when the crater itself is only a few metres wide.

This search compared old and new CTX images of the same ground automatically, pixel by pixel, and kept only dark spots that appeared between two dates and are missing from the published catalogue of 1,203 dated new craters (Daubar et al. 2022) and from the camera teams’ own target lists. All but one also lie outside every footprint of the high-resolution HiRISE camera, which photographed candidate 4 by chance. Thousands of image pairs were screened and almost everything flagged was a look-alike (shadows of old craters under a different sun, dark streaks on slopes and dunes, haze, or alignment errors); the candidates below survived every check.

Caveat. These are unconfirmed candidates, not discoveries. Each one passed every test that could be applied to the existing images, but only a new, sharper image (ideally from HiRISE) can show whether a fresh crater sits at the centre of the spot. The orbiter’s camera teams screen new images routinely and may already know about some of these. “Not in the record” means not in the public archives as of their latest releases: CTX images through May 2026 and HiRISE images through August 2026. One further catalogue, of new impacts near the InSight lander (Bickel et al. 2025), could not be downloaded; of candidates 4 to 14, numbers 5, 6, 7 and 10 lie in its area, and for them it can only be inferred that it does not list them.
