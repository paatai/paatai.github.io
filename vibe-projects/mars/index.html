Possible new impact sites on Mars

NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed much of Mars with its Context Camera (CTX) several times since 2006, and when a small meteorite hits dusty ground it blows the bright dust away and leaves a dark spot that CTX can see, even when the crater itself is only a few metres wide.

This search compared old and new CTX images of the same ground automatically, pixel by pixel, and kept only dark spots that appeared between two dates and are missing from the published catalogue of 1,203 dated new craters (Daubar et al. 2022) and from the camera teams’ own target lists. The hunt is finished. It found 166 leads: 80 strong, 81 promising and 5 weak. All of them are unconfirmed, and most are probably small new impact craters missing from the public catalogues. All but eight of the candidates below also lie outside every footprint of the high-resolution HiRISE camera: it photographed candidates 4, 21, 57, 69, 85 and 94 by chance after their spots appeared, and candidates 100 and 106 only before. Thousands of image pairs were screened and almost everything flagged was a look-alike (shadows of old craters under a different sun, dark streaks on slopes and dunes, haze, or alignment errors).

Thirty-five of the candidates (numbers 19, 20, 21, 23, 25, 28, 31, 34, 36, 37, 40, 41, 42, 47, 50, 51, 53, 56, 57, 58, 59, 63, 64, 67, 68, 69, 71, 72, 74, 77, 78, 79, 80, 84 and 85) then went through a second, deliberately sceptical review. It downloaded every image again, tested whether each earlier image would have shown the spot, and also searched two more catalogues of new impacts (Podobnaya 2026; Wagstaff et al. 2022). None of the 35 turned out to be already known, and each of their pages shows its check.

Each candidate’s number is its lead number in the hunt, the number printed on its figures, so the list follows the order in which the search found them. The positions, dates, sizes and confidences on every page are those of the hunt’s final list, candidates.csv, which also gives the full evidence for each lead and the 339 candidates the hunt rejected or found to be already known.

Caveat. These are unconfirmed candidates, not discoveries. Each one passed every test that could be applied to the existing images, but only a new, sharper image (ideally from HiRISE) can show whether a fresh crater sits at the centre of the spot. The orbiter’s camera teams screen new images routinely and may already know about some of these. “Not in the record” means not in the public archives as of their latest releases: CTX images through May 2026 and HiRISE images through August 2026. One further catalogue, of new impacts near the InSight lander (Bickel et al. 2025), could not be downloaded; of the candidates here, numbers 2, 5, 9, 11, 16, 30, 34, 40, 48, 52, 59, 74, 83, 86, 89, 94, 99, 102, 106, 108, 115, 117, 120, 123, 128, 134, 139, 142, 157, 159 and 164 lie in its area, within 50° of the lander. Numbers 30, 40, 59, 102, 106, 134 and 142 formed after the period it covers. Every impact it lists has a HiRISE image, so the others are probably not among them, except perhaps number 94, which HiRISE photographed in 2021.
