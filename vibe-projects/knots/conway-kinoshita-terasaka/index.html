The Conway knot and the Kinoshita–Terasaka knot

Watch the video (MP4, 4 MB)

Silent animation

Why it is remarkable. These two 11-crossing knots are mutants: cut one along a sphere that meets it in four points (a Conway sphere), turn the inside half a turn, and reglue, and you get the other. Mutants are notoriously hard to tell apart. Both knots have Alexander polynomial 1, the same as the unknot (Kinoshita and Terasaka constructed their knot in 1957 for this reason), and they also share the Jones and HOMFLY-PT polynomials and the hyperbolic volume. Riley distinguished them in 1971, and Gabai showed in 1986 that they even have different genus: 3 for the Conway knot, 2 for Kinoshita–Terasaka. The related problem is sliceness: does the knot bound a smooth disk in the 4-dimensional ball? Kinoshita–Terasaka does. The Conway knot was the last knot with fewer than 13 crossings whose answer was unknown, until Lisa Piccirillo proved in 2020 that it is not slice.

What the animation shows. On the left is the Conway knot (K11n34 in the knot tables), with a sphere that meets it in exactly four points. The knot is cut at the four points, the ball inside is turned half a turn, swapping the points in pairs, and the ends are reglued. The result is the Kinoshita–Terasaka knot (K11n42). This step is labelled in the film as cut and reglue, not an isotopy: it is not a motion of the knot, and it produces a different knot. The two knots agree outside the sphere; inside, the tangle is turned over. The film then compares them: same Alexander, Jones and HOMFLY-PT polynomials, same Khovanov homology, same hyperbolic volume 11.2191…, but genus 3 versus genus 2, and only Kinoshita–Terasaka is slice.

S. Kinoshita & H. Terasaka, On unions of knots, Osaka Math. J. 9 (1957); R. Riley (1971); D. Gabai (1986); L. Piccirillo, The Conway knot is not slice, Ann. of Math. 191 (2020), 581–591.
