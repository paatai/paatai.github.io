The Culprit

Watch the video (MP4, 5 MB)

Silent animation

Why it is remarkable. This 10-crossing drawing is the unknot, an ordinary untangled loop, yet no Reidemeister move makes it simpler: no move removes a crossing, and no type III move applies. So every untangling must first add crossings, going up to at least 11. The diagram is due to Ken Millett; Kauffman and Lambropoulou named it the Culprit. It illustrates the unknot recognition problem: decide whether a drawing shows the unknot. Reidemeister (1927), and independently Alexander and Briggs (1926), showed that any two drawings of the same knot are related by the three Reidemeister moves, but not how many moves or extra crossings may be needed. Haken gave the first algorithm in 1961. Hass, Lagarias and Pippenger proved in 1999 that the problem is in NP (when the answer is yes, there is a short proof); Kuperberg proved it is also in co-NP assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and Lackenby proved this without that assumption. Lackenby also proved that an unknot diagram with c crossings can be untangled in at most (236c)¹¹ Reidemeister moves (2015), and in 2021 announced an algorithm that runs in quasi-polynomial time.

What the animation shows. The untangling takes 11 moves and never has more than 11 crossings: a type I move adds a kink (11 crossings), four type III moves slide strands over crossings, five type II moves pull strands apart two crossings at a time, and a last type I move untwists the final crossing. No crossings are left: it was the unknot all along. A counter shows the number of crossings and the most so far. (The untangling published by Kauffman and Lambropoulou takes ten moves but goes up to 12 crossings.) Every step between frames is checked: no strand passes through another.

Briggs, Ann. of Math. 28 (1926); W. Haken, Acta Math. 105 (1961); J. Hass, J. C. Lagarias & N. Pippenger, J. ACM 46 (1999); G. Kuperberg, Adv. Math. 256 (2014); M. Lackenby, Ann. of Math. 182 (2015) and Adv. Math. 387 (2021).
