Minimal surfaces

Played with AI (Grok bot) to learn something about minimal surfaces. Take any point of a minimal surface and a small enough patch around it: that patch has the smallest area among all surfaces with the same boundary curve. A soap film on a wire is the physical picture.

Here are some of the unique examples in 3D. Click a surface to open its narrated animation (each opens on its own page). The terms in bold are explained in the dictionary below.

  1. Nadirashvili’s complete minimal disk inside a ball — a complete minimal surface can be bounded.
  2. A complete minimal surface dense in space — one complete minimal surface can come arbitrarily close to every point of a region.
  3. Schoen’s gyroid — a triply periodic surface splitting space into two labyrinths, with no straight lines and no mirror planes.
  4. Costa’s surface — the first new complete embedded minimal surface of finite topology since the catenoid and helicoid: genus one, three ends.
  5. The genus-one helicoid — a helicoid with one handle.
  6. Riemann’s minimal examples — every horizontal slice is a circle or a line; infinitely many flat ends.

Intuitive dictionary

Each term is defined exactly, followed by equivalent ways to think about it. Surfaces live in ordinary 3-dimensional space ℝ³ and are smooth.

Area-minimizing patch
A piece of surface with a boundary curve whose area is the smallest among all surfaces having that same boundary curve.
Minimal surface
A surface such that around every point, every small enough patch is area-minimizing: it has the least area among all surfaces with the same boundary curve. Equivalent characterizations:
  • No first-order gain: any small deformation that keeps a patch’s boundary fixed does not change the area to first order (the area is “stationary”, like a function at a critical point).
  • Mean curvature zero: at every point the two principal curvatures are equal and opposite, κ₁ = −κ₂. So at every point the surface is either flat or saddle-shaped, bending up in one direction exactly as much as it bends down in the perpendicular direction.
  • Soap films: a soap film on a wire frame is (to very good approximation) a minimal surface, because surface tension pulls it to reduce area.
Warning: “minimal” does not mean the whole surface has least area. Only small enough patches are guaranteed to. For example, a long thin catenoid between two rings is a minimal surface, yet it does not have least area for its two rings (see the split-screen animation).
Principal curvatures, mean curvature, Gaussian curvature
Cut the surface at a point by planes containing the normal line; each cut is a curve with a signed curvature (1/radius of the best-fitting circle). The largest and smallest of these are the principal curvatures κ₁ and κ₂. The mean curvature is their average (κ₁ + κ₂)/2; the Gaussian curvature is their product κ₁κ₂. On a minimal surface the Gaussian curvature is ≤ 0 everywhere. By Gauss’s Theorema Egregium, Gaussian curvature can be measured by an ant living on the surface, so bending a surface without stretching it never changes its Gaussian curvature.
Intrinsic distance
The distance between two points measured by walking on the surface: the shortest length of a path that stays on the surface (not the straight-line distance through space).
Complete
You cannot walk off the surface in finite distance: every path on the surface that runs off toward its “edge” (eventually leaving every finite piece of the surface for good) has infinite length. Equivalently, the surface is complete as a metric space for its intrinsic distance; equivalently (Hopf–Rinow), every geodesic (“straight walk” on the surface) can be continued forever. Examples: the plane, the catenoid and the helicoid are complete; an open disk is not (you reach its rim in finite distance). Complete does not mean unbounded in space: Nadirashvili’s surface is complete but fits inside a ball.
Bounded
The surface fits inside some ball in space.
Immersed vs. embedded
Immersed: near each of its own points the surface is a smooth surface, but different parts are allowed to pass through each other. Embedded: it never crosses or touches itself; every point of space is on at most one sheet.
Properly embedded
Embedded, and every bounded region of space meets only a finite (compact) piece of the surface; the surface does not pile up anywhere. Properly embedded surfaces are automatically complete. A surface that is dense in a region (below) is never proper.
Dense in a region
The surface comes arbitrarily close to every point of the region: every small ball in the region, however tiny, meets the surface. Equivalently, the closure of the surface is the whole region.
Orientable
The surface has two sides: one can choose a unit normal vector at every point that varies continuously. A Möbius strip is not orientable.
Simply connected
Every closed loop on the surface can be shrunk to a point without leaving the surface. For the surfaces here (no boundary, not compact) this means topologically a plane. The plane and the helicoid are simply connected; the catenoid is not (a loop around its neck cannot be shrunk).
Genus
The number of handles. Exactly: for an orientable surface, the largest number of disjoint simple closed curves along which you can cut all at once without the surface falling apart into pieces. A sphere, plane, catenoid or helicoid has genus 0; a torus, and Costa’s surface, have genus 1.
Ends
The different ways of going off to infinity on the surface. Exactly: cut out a compact (finite) part of the surface and count the leftover pieces that still run off toward the surface’s “edge” (pieces that are not themselves contained in a compact part); the number of ends is the largest count you can get as the cut-out part grows. The plane and the helicoid have one end, the catenoid two, Costa’s surface three (two like catenoid ends, one flat, i.e. asymptotic to a plane), Riemann’s examples infinitely many.
Finite topology
The surface is a compact surface with finitely many points removed; equivalently, it has finite genus and finitely many ends. Each removed point corresponds to one end.
Periodic (singly, doubly, triply)
The surface is unchanged by a translation (a shift) of space. Singly periodic: invariant under shifts by one vector (and its multiples), like Riemann’s examples. Triply periodic: invariant under shifts in three independent directions, like a crystal, as for the gyroid and Schwarz’s P and D surfaces.
Plane, catenoid, helicoid
The catenoid is the surface of revolution of a hanging-chain curve (catenary) x = a cosh(z/a); it is the shape of a soap film between two nearby parallel rings. The helicoid is swept out by a horizontal line that rotates at constant speed while it moves up a vertical axis, like a spiral staircase or screw: (s cos t, s sin t, ct). Together with the plane these were the only known complete embedded minimal surfaces of finite topology until Costa’s surface (1982).
Associate family; Bonnet angle
Any simply connected piece of a minimal surface can be bent continuously, through minimal surfaces, by an angle θ from 0° to 360° without stretching: all lengths on the surface (and so the Gaussian curvature) stay the same. At θ = 90° one gets the conjugate surface; for example, a piece of the catenoid bends into a piece of the helicoid. Schwarz’s D surface bends into Schwarz’s P surface at 90°, and at the Bonnet angle ≈ 38.01° (from D) it is the gyroid. The other surfaces in between do not close up into embedded surfaces; the gyroid is the only exception.
Gauss map; Weierstrass representation
The Gauss map sends each point of the surface to its unit normal vector, a point on the unit sphere. The Weierstrass representation writes every minimal surface by explicit integrals of two complex-analytic (holomorphic) ingredients: the Gauss map (seen in the complex plane via stereographic projection) and a “height differential”. All the exact animations here are computed this way.
Exact / numerical / schematic (labels on the videos)
Exact: drawn from a closed-form formula for the real surface. Numerical: an exact formula whose few free constants are computed numerically. Schematic: an illustration of the idea, not the surface itself.
